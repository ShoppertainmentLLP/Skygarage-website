A vibration through the steering wheel at 110 km/h on Sheikh Zayed Road is the most common
complaint in this category, and it is usually a few grams of lead in the wrong place.

Every wheel and tyre assembly has a heavy spot. At parking speeds it makes no difference. At
100 km/h that wheel is turning about 15 times a second, and a heavy spot of even 20 grams becomes
a force large enough to shake the whole steering column. Balancing finds where the heavy spot is
and puts a counterweight opposite it.

## Reading the symptom

Where you feel it tells you which end of the car to look at. Vibration through the steering wheel
is almost always a front wheel. Vibration through the seat and the floor is a rear one. If it
comes on at a particular speed and smooths out again above it, that is a classic imbalance
signature.

If it only happens under braking, that is not balance at all — that is a warped disc or a
sticking caliper, and it is a brake job.

## Why weights fall off here

Alloy wheels take adhesive weights on the inside of the barrel. In a Dubai car park in July a
wheel sits well above 60°C, and cheap adhesive gives up. A car that was smooth for a year and is
suddenly not may simply have lost a weight on the way to work.

That is also why cleaning the rim before fitting a new weight matters. Adhesive over old
adhesive, sand or corrosion is a weight that will not stay on.

## When balancing does not fix it

If the vibration survives a proper four-wheel balance, the balance was not the problem. A buckled
rim from a pothole, a tyre with a shifted internal belt, a worn hub bearing or a bent driveshaft
all produce something that feels similar.

A road-force balancer is the machine that separates these — it presses a loaded roller against
the tyre while it spins, which reproduces what happens on the road and exposes faults a free
spin cannot see. It costs more, and it is the right next step rather than balancing the same
wheels a second time.
