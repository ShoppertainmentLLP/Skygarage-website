Almost everyone arrives at diagnostics the same way: a light came on, the car feels fine, and
nobody can say whether it is a AED 200 sensor or a AED 6,000 problem.

Answering that is the entire service. Everything else follows from it.

## What a scan really produces

A fault code is a complaint, logged by a module, about a measurement it did not expect. It names
a circuit. It does not name the broken part.

The most common example: a lean-running code on the engine. That can be a vacuum leak, a failing
mass airflow sensor, a weak fuel pump, a dirty injector, an exhaust leak upstream of the sensor,
or the sensor itself. Six causes, one code, and a very wide price range.

A workshop that reads the code and immediately quotes a part has skipped the actual work. One
that reads the code, looks at live data, checks the freeze-frame conditions and tests the circuit
has done the job you are paying for.

## Why the whole car, not just the engine

A generic reader plugged into the port sees emissions-related engine codes. That is the standard
it was built for.

Meanwhile the transmission module, the ABS module, the body control module and half a dozen
others are storing their own faults — and on a modern car most of the annoying problems live
there. Intermittent electrical faults, a window that stops working in heat, a warning that
appears only on cold start.

## The flashing light

Worth knowing, because it is the one genuine emergency in this category. A steady check engine
light means get it looked at. A flashing one means an active misfire is putting unburnt fuel into
the catalytic converter, which can destroy it in minutes.

If it is flashing, stop and call for recovery. It is the cheapest decision available at that
moment.

## Take the report with you

The diagnosis belongs to you. A written report — codes, live data, the tested conclusion — is
what lets you get comparable quotes for the repair instead of accepting the first number.
